My research agenda covers three main questions:

### 1. How do early-career workers cope with uncertainty in labor markets?
**Cooper, Zosia. “Beyond the Paycheck: Hope Reallocation and Young Americans’ Entry into Financial Markets.” Preparing for submission to *American Sociological Review*.**  
Management scholars have documented an increasingly insecure employment relationship, with the decline of internal career ladders leaving workers to navigate growing career uncertainty; employers’ integration of generative AI into their workstreams has only amplified this uncertainty. In my job market paper, I show how workers strategically use money and finance to cope with uncertainty in labor markets. Using data from the triennial National Financial Capability Study, I first show a large surge in financial investment in the 18–24-year-old age group over the past 12 years, which is unique to this age group. Then, using data from 94 interviews with 60 advanced college students and recent graduates, I consider the demand-side mechanisms behind this surge. I find that young workers invest against the possibility that work will fail to deliver their desired futures in the middle class. Drawing on the literature on the microfoundations of institutions, I characterize this use of investment as *hope reallocation*: a process through which people choose to rely on alternative institutions to deliver desired future outcomes. Reallocation maintains the individual’s forward momentum toward the desired future when a traditional institution loses credibility. I explore the justifications that make this reallocation possible and consider cases in which neither traditional nor alternative institutions are accessible. Overall, I argue that young workers are strategically reducing their reliance on organizational employment, and that technology accelerates this shift, providing both a motive (AI-driven job insecurity) and the means (easier access to alternatives such as investment).

**Cooper, Zosia. “Same Money, Different Message: A Comparative Analysis of YouTube Career and Finance Advice” (working title). Data analysis in progress; targeting *Administrative Science Quarterly*.**  
In this paper, I explore how identity shapes the interplay between work and wealth-building. I examine online personal finance advice, which my young respondents follow closely as they start to invest. This advice circulates lay economic theories about risk, returns, and the reliability of work, and represents widely held perceptions of appropriate action, especially when analyzed at scale. I conduct a computational analysis of video transcripts from personal finance YouTube channels in order to achieve this scale. Preliminarily, I find that personal finance content creators are more likely to highlight wealth-building as a means to hedge against risk when addressing women, when compared to men. They are also more likely to encourage people of color (as compared to white viewers) to accumulate visible wealth in order to model possibility for the next generation. These appeals may lead to variation in how young people combine low-barrier-to-entry markets and organizational careers, based on their desire to overcome challenges that they believe are specific to their identity group.

### 2. How is the meaning of contemporary work changing?
**Cooper, Zosia. “How Early-Career Trade-Offs Shape the Meaning of Work.” Preparing for submission to *Organization Science*.**  
As workers’ attachments to organizations and careers change, theories on the meaning of work may require an update. Research tells us workers who find their work meaningful are highly committed to their careers, but we don’t yet know how and for whom meaningfulness is changing. In this paper from my dissertation, I track these developments among young professionals. In order to increase their chances on the job market after college, young people commit to career paths relatively early. For many, this involves trading off between a meaningful path and a financially secure path—which they see as mutually exclusive. Using data from longitudinal interviews, I find that these trade-offs cause young workers to form rigid notions about work and what role it plays in their lives that follow them into the workforce and through multiple roles and organizations, and that shape quitting intentions and career advancement. Those who choose secure careers over careers they are passionate about define themselves as people who find meaning outside of work rather than within work. As a result, they eschew opportunities for advancement that would require them to devote more time to their work. They also move between organizations in pursuit of better work-life balance even before they have caregiving responsibilities. Because these orientations form before organizational entry and persist across employers, this finding contributes to research on socialization and talent development, as well as to evidence that work centrality is diminishing.

**Scruggs, Jared and Zosia Cooper. “When Workers Break the Fourth Wall: Contentious Frames and the Battle over Work Culture.” Preparing for submission to *Academy of Management Review*.**  
Online content about work is often negative and even contentious: popular memes exclaim that “work will not love you back,” and “if you died today, your job would be posted tomorrow.” Additionally, online movements against overwork and corporate employment relationships (e.g., quiet quitting, lying flat) have gained momentum in recent years. In this theoretical paper, we build on theories of meaningful work and stigma to theorize about the interpersonal and organizational conditions under which contentious ideas about work spread within organizations, and when dissent surfaces as voice or as collective action.

### 3. How do professionals experience and manage political tension in their fields? 
Among the forces reshaping contemporary work, political polarization is one of the least studied. Polarization manifests in a variety of interpersonal contexts and may unsettle shared understandings of what a profession is for, requiring members to renegotiate the meaning of their work. In a series of papers with Amy Binder and Jeffrey Kidder, I leverage interviews with libertarian academics, documentary evidence, and historical accounts of libertarian organizing in academia to shed light on this broader question.

**Cooper, Zosia, Amy J. Binder, and Jeffrey L. Kidder. “Maintaining Ideological Niches in the Professions.” Preparing for submission to *Journal of Management*.**    
In this paper, we develop the concept of *professional polysemy*. Polysemic professions are those in which there is more than one way to understand what it means to be a member of that profession. This multiplicity of meanings enables conservative professionals to identify with professional values that align with their political values, even in fields that they see as left-leaning. In academia, this means identifying with traditional values of intellectual rigor and open-mindedness, while largely rejecting progressive academic values of inclusion and equity. We explore the organizational and work settings in which these traditional professional values may be enacted and contrasted with progressive ones, and argue that this enactment redirects conservative professionals away from collective action to change professional practice.

**Cooper, Zosia, Amy J. Binder, and Jeffrey L. Kidder. 2024. “Keeping Libertarianism Alive in the Academy: Organizations, Scholars, and the Idea Pipeline.” Socius 10:23780231241287949. doi:10.1177/23780231241287949**  
We examine how actors outside of academia built an “idea pipeline,” adapting organizational forms and processes that already existed in the field to maintain otherwise unpopular streams of scholarship in the social sciences and humanities.
